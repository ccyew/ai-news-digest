## 4. Is it legal to train AI models on copyrighted books? It’s complicated

**Source:** TechCrunch AI | **Published:** 2026-08-23 15:00 UTC

Legal experts tell TechCrunch that the question of whether AI training on copyrighted books is lawful remains unsettled, with courts interpreting a copyright law last updated in 1976. Judge William Alsup's ruling that led to Anthropic's $1.5 billion settlement actually found the AI training itself legal — likening it to a writer studying literature — and penalized the company only for pirating books from shadow libraries. Other cases point to a pattern where courts reject fair use when the AI product directly competes with the source, as in Thomson Reuters' win over Ross Intelligence, while Thaler v. Perlmutter held that fully AI-generated works can't be copyrighted; most major suits are still pending.

## 5. 'AI refuser' quit her dream job, and hopes others follow

**Source:** Hacker News AI | **Published:** 2026-08-23 10:54 UTC

Gabrielle Boyle, the AFL's participation manager for northern NSW, resigned in June after the league told her in writing she had no right to opt out of Microsoft 365 Copilot, which was switched on across the organisation three days later with less than two weeks' notice. The AFL argued Copilot only accesses files she could already open and isn't meaningfully different from existing work surveillance, while Boyle cited data centre energy use, cognitive harms and AI risk; her union, the Fair Work Commission and LawAccess NSW all said they couldn't help. The case highlights gaps in Australian law, where employee records are exempt from privacy protections and Fair Work consultation duties apply only to changes with significant effects — though NSW's new Digital Work Systems Act, untested on tools like Copilot, imposes safety duties on employers over digital systems.
